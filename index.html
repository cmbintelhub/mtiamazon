Who is the competitor?

It depends on the front: Walmart, Temu and Shein in retail, Azure and Google in cloud, Google and Meta in ads, Netflix and Disney in streaming. No single list.

Types of Innovation: Amazon

Management of Technology and Innovation · Group 7

Authors: André Abbud, Giada Patellani, Júlia Simão, Maria Luísa Stanisci, Nicola Lano, Paolo Vasquez and Sara Alssabak. The roadmap on the home page is our recommendation built on this analysis; where it differs from the report (the 2026 capex update and the healthcare freeze), the chatbot explains why.

1. Company Analysis of the Current Status

Amazon cannot be viewed as a typical retailer because it is a complex ecosystem consisting of such components as e-commerce, third-party marketplace, logistics, cloud computing, digital advertising, subscriptions, media, devices, and artificial intelligence. The competitive advantage of Amazon comes from its ability to integrate these various kinds of business because each component of the company supports other elements and allows them to work effectively. For example, retail creates traffic and transactions; Prime creates loyalty; Marketplace increases product assortment without holding all inventory by Amazon; logistics creates convenience; advertising monetizes purchase intention; and finally, AWS creates the technological platform and provides the main stream of profits.

Thus, in 2025 Amazon had net sales of US$716.9 billion that shows 12% year-to-year growth. The sales of AWS were equal to US$128.7 billion, and operating income of Amazon was US$80 billion. As we can see, retail still provides the highest revenue, but cloud computing and advertising play an important role in creating profits and supporting innovations.

1.1 Market Profile

Amazon competes in multiple interdependent markets. The company’s key business lines include:

  • Retail via direct sales of products from Amazon;
  • A third-party marketplace connecting sellers independently from Amazon and customers;
  • Fulfillment and logistics operations, especially Fulfillment by Amazon;
  • Cloud computing and other digital services provided via AWS;
  • Advertising;
  • Subscription offerings via Amazon Prime;
  • Entertainment and media via Prime Video, Amazon Music and Audible;
  • Consumer goods including Kindle, Fire TV and Echo;
  • Healthcare, satellite internet and AI generation are emerging areas of business.

The retail market is a multi-sided platform where Amazon acts as an intermediary for end customers, third party sellers, advertisers and other service providers who all create value for each other. More sellers lead to increased diversity and price competition that attracts more customers. Higher numbers of customers make the platform more valuable for sellers and advertisers. Network effects take place here.

From the point of view of the customers, the key value proposition offered by Amazon includes wide selection, low prices, convenience, reliability and fast delivery. Search, recommendations, reviews, easy payments and returns decrease the cost and uncertainty associated with purchases. The membership program Prime combines most of those features into a subscription service. For sellers, Amazon offers instant access to customers, payment system, advertising instruments and logistics services. Rather than build all this infrastructure from scratch themselves – website, traffic generation, payment system and logistics system, sellers have complimentary access to them via Amazon platform. From Coasean perspective, Amazon reduces transaction costs associated with search, information, trust, payment and delivery.

The competition on Amazon platforms is very diversified. In retail, Amazon fights against Walmart, Target, Alibaba, Mercado Libre and regional marketplaces, in addition to low-cost competitors like Temu and Shein. Shopify is an example of competitive approach of another kind that helps brands sell without being on central marketplaces. AWS in cloud computing is mainly competing with Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud. Amazon Advertising competes against Google, Meta and retail media networks. Entertainment business of Amazon competes against Netflix, Disney and streaming platforms.

Thus, Amazon’s competition cannot be summarized in a single list. It is attacked piece by piece while its own pieces are interconnected thanks to technology and customer base.

1.2 Firm and Organization

According to Penrose, Amazon can be defined as productive resources and knowledge which can be combined to produce new businesses. Initially created capabilities used for solving internal problems have been successfully repurposed into an external product on many occasions. The best example is AWS, which was built to serve Amazon’s own digital processes and then turned into a cloud provider for others.

The main resources available to Amazon are:

  • Brand and reputation for convenience;
  • Large number of customers and Prime subscribers;
  • Data on transactions, demand and intent to purchase;
  • Marketplace and sellers network;
  • Fulfillment centers and last mile logistics;
  • AWS infrastructure and developers network;
  • Artificial intelligence, robotics and cloud;
  • Innovative hardware (Trainium, Inferentia, Graviton);
  • Technologies (Trainium, Inferentia and Graviton);
  • Knowledge within the organizational structure.

Formally, Amazon is a hierarchical company with CEO being Andy Jassy while Jeff Bezos acts as the Executive Chairman. Corporate goals, allocation of capital and strategy development are the responsibility of the CEO and senior leadership team. Below that level, Amazon is organized into business units (e.g. AWS, Worldwide Amazon Stores, Advertising, Devices & Services, Operations). But what makes Amazon distinctive is that it incorporates this hierarchy into a decentralized approach to execution. Small teams are granted ownership of a certain product, service or customer issue. The two-pizza teams and single-threading of leadership are intended to lower coordination costs and make sure there is one accountable individual for an idea.

This results in a mixed structure. Power and capital allocation strategies stay centralized, but product and operation decisions are outsourced to teams close to customers. Amazon coordinates those levels via standardized mechanisms instead of constant top-down coordination.

Amazon's main mechanisms of management are:

  • Working Backwards – teams start from a customer problem and create a PR/FAQ before coming up with a solution;
  • Six-page narrative documents instead of presentation slides;
  • Type 1 and Type 2 decisions that separate hard to undo decisions from reversible experiments;
  • “Disagree and commit” that stimulates debate before action;
  • Weekly Business Reviews and input metrics;
  • Leadership Principles used when hiring people and making decisions on a daily basis.

These mechanisms help Amazon to have a somewhat stable culture within such a huge and diverse organization.

1.3 Corporate Strategy

The strategy of Amazon involves an ecosystem approach, focused on customer convenience, scale, and continuous investment of resources. The corporate logic of Amazon is explained with the help of Amazon’s flywheel: lower prices and convenience draw customers; more customers attract sellers; more sellers add to the selection; higher volumes allow for better infrastructure usage and lower unit costs; and lower costs can be further invested in prices, delivery, and innovations.

The strategy of the company involves related diversification and vertical integration. Although the business lines of Amazon may seem disparate, they depend upon the same technological and organizational resources. Retail, logistics, advertising, Prime, and AWS all benefit from the same capabilities in data, cloud computing, artificial intelligence, payments, and customer relations.

However, Amazon has continuously brought into-house activities that were strategically important. Thus, it has developed its fulfillment centers, delivery network, cloud infrastructure and semiconductor design, gaining better control over costs, capacity, performance, and customer experience. Nevertheless, it continues to remain open in those cases when participation in the ecosystem generates additional value, such as third-party sellers, developers, content creators, delivery partners, and AWS clients.

This strategy can thus be summarized as follows:

  1. Maintaining ownership of the customer interface;
  2. Driving efficiencies by way of automation and logistics densification within regions;
  3. Ensuring that AWS maintains its dominance in cloud computing and AI infrastructure;
  4. Using synergies to enter adjacent businesses.

The primary strategic challenge here is that Amazon needs to simultaneously ensure efficiencies in mature business processes as well as experiments in uncertain new markets. Too much control may hinder innovation, but too much decentralization may cause duplication and conflicts.

1.4 External Environment

The external environment of Amazon is being affected by technology, regulation, society, and competition changes.

Technologically, the development of generative AI is impacting the cloud computing sector and product discovery. The shopping process might start through conversational agents as opposed to searching for products from a search box in a traditional marketplace. It poses a big threat to Amazon as the company earns money through ads and needs to control the attention and intentions of customers. At the same time, it creates opportunities for Amazon to make use of Rufus, Alexa+, Bedrock and own AI chips in order to provide more personalized services.

Warehouse robotics and automation are helping increase the efficiency of fulfillment, while drones and autonomous deliveries remain more emerging technologies. In terms of cloud computing, the key limitations are the availability of advanced chips, energy, capacity of data centers and talent.

Socially, people expect to receive goods quickly and inexpensively with easy returns and personalization. Young customers are extremely price-sensitive but they often find new products from social and video sites. In terms of regulations, Amazon continues to come under fire. The case filed by the FTC on Amazon concerns the company's policies regarding Marketplace and the treatment of sellers. The Amazon Marketplace and Amazon Advertising within Europe have to meet their obligations under the Digital Markets Act. Labor laws, warehouse safety, data privacy, and artificial intelligence regulation contribute to the cost of operations of Amazon owing to its size.

Therefore, Amazon faces a complicated situation where it is proactive in cloud computing, automation, and digital commerce, but at the same time it is reactive regarding legislation and other issues.

1.5 Value Chain and Technological Interfaces

The value chain at Amazon includes physical, digital, and technological operations.

As far as inputs are concerned, Amazon depends on suppliers of products, third-party merchants, providers of technology, manufacturers of semiconductors, providers of energy, providers of content, and logistics firms. The firm procures hardware items including GPUs, servers, networking equipment, and other devices. This includes customized computer chips and proprietary warehouse systems.

In retail, Amazon orchestrates product assortment, pricing, inventories, payments, fulfillment, and shipping. Third-party sellers have the ability to perform fulfillment themselves or use FBA service that ties them to Amazon’s network of warehouses and logistics capabilities. In AWS, Amazon converts computing, storage, networking, databases, and AI technologies into modulated services accessible via APIs.

Key technological interfaces in this value chain include:

  • APIs and cloud services;
  • Recommendation and search engines;
  • Selling and advertising platforms;
  • Payment and authentication systems;
  • Warehouse and inventory management systems;
  • Machine learning-based forecasting and pricing models;
  • Robotic and fulfillment technologies;
  • Applications and AI assistants.

The complementary resources of Amazon are particularly significant in the context of Teece’s model. While many technologies may be imitated, the entire bundle of features such as Prime, Marketplace, brand trust, customer information, distribution network, AWS, and large number of developers and sellers cannot easily be copied. This allows the firm to benefit economically from innovations even if they are not patentable.

1.6 Dynamic Capabilities

Amazon illustrates the three dynamic capabilities proposed by Teece: sensing, seizing and transforming.

Sensing: Amazon senses the opportunities via customer feedback, transaction information, technology, experimentation and direct interaction with sellers and AWS customers. "Working Backwards" allows teams to identify customers' unfulfilled needs before choosing a technological solution.

Seizing: After sensing an opportunity, Amazon may mobilize financial resources, technology, specialized teams and complementary assets to create and implement the solution. AWS, Prime and Marketplace reflect the ability of Amazon to turn opportunities into successful businesses.

Transforming: Amazon constantly transforms its assets. The examples are transformation of internal infrastructure into AWS, going from relying on logistics companies to having its own delivery network, implementing robots in fulfillment and creating chips to become less dependent on outside suppliers.

Such capabilities are supported by a high maturity level in the following areas:

  • Development capability: cloud services, AI, devices, robotics and custom silicon;
  • Operational capability: fulfillment, inventory management and last mile;
  • Management capability: standard mechanisms of planning, decision-making and experimentation;
  • Transaction capability: organizing interactions between consumers, sellers, developers and advertisers.

Amazon’s main competence is consequently not one particular product or technology, but its ability to combine these four capabilities and scale their results across several markets.

1.7 Firm Boundaries

Amazon's boundaries are wide yet discriminating. Amazon incorporates those business activities that help the company gain a competitive edge through control, integration, scale or accumulated knowledge. Such areas as cloud infrastructure, fulfillment, data storage and processing, platform governance, and semiconductors are seen by Amazon as integral parts of its competencies.

Meanwhile, other business activities fall outside the company's boundaries, but still belong to its ecosystem. Marketplace third-party sellers are responsible for most of the products available; delivery service providers carry out part of last mile deliveries; developers use AWS; and other companies generate content, hardware and unique technologies.

Thus, Amazon functions as both a company and a platform provider and an ecosystem orchestrator. By doing so, Amazon can take advantage of variety and entrepreneurship beyond the company's boundaries while still controlling critical interfaces, standards, customer access and transaction data.

The danger here is that Amazon's boundaries can grow to be excessively wide. Too many unrelated or loosely related markets would mean growing coordination costs and managerial challenges as well as higher capital expenses. The problem is not whether Amazon can enter another market, but whether this activity is consistent with Amazon's competencies and ecosystem.

1.8 Main Gaps and Problems

However, despite all the advantages, Amazon has some critical internal and external challenges.

First, large-scale organizations have issues with coordination. Autonomous teams can duplicate each other in terms of technologies used, fight over resources or follow different strategies from the corporate one. The standardization process mitigates such challenges but brings bureaucracy at the same time.

Second, Amazon’s product portfolio demands big and constant capital expenses. The implementation of the necessary infrastructure in AI, data centers, logistics and satellites requires significant expenditures given the technological and demand uncertainty. Such expenses belong to Type 1 decisions and are hard to be undone.

Third, Amazon’s competitive advantage in generative AI is controversial. AWS is a leader in terms of infrastructure; however, Microsoft and Google have strong ecosystems of models, software, and enterprises. Amazon has to make sure that investments into Bedrock, Anthropic and proprietary chips result in differentiation rather than capacity addition.

Fourth, the traditional Amazon shopping experience is threatened by social commerce and external AI. If customers start their search somewhere else, Amazon can lose control over product discovery and thus the advertisement activity built around it. Moreover, legal and reputational risks can constrain the utilization of seller information, self-preferencing by the platform, bundling, and personalized advertising. The labor situation, sustainability, and even warehouse safety may also be the possible areas of such risk.

Lastly, Amazon needs to find a balance between efficiency and experimentation. Although its approach is very successful in dealing with tangible issues, innovation may sometimes require some flexibility and uncertainty.

1.9 Innovation Management Profile

The innovation management structure of Amazon is both entrepreneurial, managerial and ecosystem oriented.

It is entrepreneurial since it generates novel products, processes, markets and organizational forms, matching up with Schumpeterian notion of new combinations. These innovations include Kindle, AWS, Prime, robotically driven fulfillment centers, marketplace, as well as proprietary AI chips.

It is managerial, since the process of innovation is institutionalized and relies on repeated process rather than on creativity alone. “Working Backwards”, “PR/FAQ” documents, small independent teams, metrics of input and decision-making stages transform innovation into organizational process.

Finally, Amazon is the company following the fifth generation approach to innovation, since the process of innovation happens through networks that involve customers, sellers, developers, suppliers, delivery providers, startups and AWS customers. Amazon does not conduct innovation process only within its centralized R&D division but orchestrates the ecosystem where innovation is done by external players as well. In general, Amazon may be characterized as a very mature organization in terms of innovation with a high level of development and operations as well as managerial and transactional competences. At the moment, the problem for Amazon is not in producing more innovations. The task is to coordinate an extensive number of innovation projects according to the strategy of the company and select the ones that will enhance its complementary assets.

2. Innovation Strategic Alignment

2.1 Alignment with Corporate Strategy

Amazon's corporate strategy rests on four pillars: maintaining ownership of the customer interface; driving efficiency through automation and logistics densification; ensuring AWS dominance in cloud and AI infrastructure; and using synergies to enter adjacent businesses. Innovation strategy is what carries this out: following the course's definition, it is the sum of a technological strategy (which technologies to master: AI, robotics, custom silicon) and a business strategy (where to capture value: retail, ads, cloud, subscriptions). In Dodgson's typology, Amazon runs a clearly proactive innovation strategy: it aims at technological and market leadership, combines radical and incremental innovation, accepts high-risk bets in the portfolio, and appropriates returns through complementary assets and speed rather than patents alone. Alignment is guaranteed by mechanisms, not intentions: every initiative must be born from a PR/FAQ (Working Backwards), pass through the annual OP1/OP2 planning cycle that connects team-level projects to corporate goals, and be owned by a single-threaded leader. The alignment test is the flywheel itself, a project is approved when it lowers costs, increases selection or strengthens the platform assets shared by all businesses.

2.2 Innovation Plan

The innovation plan mirrors the four strategic pillars. For the customer interface: Rufus, Alexa+ and preparation for agentic commerce (including participation in interoperability standards), so that product discovery is not lost to external AI agents. For efficiency: warehouse robotics, sortation automation and regional network densification, attacking cost-to-serve: the decisive variable against Temu, Shein and Walmart. For AWS: Trainium chip generations, data-center expansion, Bedrock and the Anthropic partnership, defending infrastructure leadership in the AI era. For adjacencies: healthcare (One Medical, Amazon Pharmacy), Project Kuiper, grocery and the low-price storefront Haul. The plan is deliberately ambidextrous (Tushman & O'Reilly): mature businesses are managed for efficiency while uncertain markets are explored through cheap, reversible experiments (Type 2 decisions), reserving slow, senior commitment for irreversible bets (Type 1).

2.3 Number and Portfolio of Innovation Projects

Following the two-dimensional typology of innovation projects seen in class, the portfolio behaves as the theory predicts: the majority of projects are incremental and developmental, and only a few are radical or discontinuous. In practice, Amazon runs thousands of team-level incremental projects per year (every two-pizza team continuously improves its product); dozens of major development programs (Rufus, Trainium 3, robotics generations, Alexa+, Haul); and a small set of discontinuous bets: Kuiper, agentic commerce, autonomous delivery (Zoox) and healthcare at scale. As a portfolio logic, this approximates the classic 70/20/10 distribution: roughly 70% of effort on improving the core, 20% on growth adjacencies, 10% on transformational bets. The historical pattern documented in class ("Exploration and Exploitation at Amazon", Table 2.1) shows this alternation of explore and exploit projects has been constant since 1994, the portfolio is a permanent feature, not a phase.

2.4 Time Horizons

The plan maps directly onto the Three Horizons of Growth framework. Horizon 1 (0–2 years, core business): retail efficiency, robotics payback, advertising growth, Rufus adoption and Haul's defense of the low end, managed for profit and cash. Horizon 2 (2–5 years, growth businesses): agentic commerce as a new sales channel, healthcare scaling, Trainium displacing third-party GPUs inside AWS, international expansion of the low-cost format, managed for market share and revenue growth. Horizon 3 (5–10 years, exploratory): Kuiper's global connectivity (which also creates future e-commerce customers), autonomous last-mile delivery, physical AI and robotics as a service, managed for learning and optionality. The essential point is simultaneity: Amazon does not sequence the horizons, it runs all three at once, which is precisely what the ambidextrous organization design (Quadrant IV in Tushman & O'Reilly) makes possible.

2.5 Required Innovation Capabilities

Each horizon demands a different mix of the four innovation capabilities. Horizon 1 depends mostly on the ordinary capabilities: Operations (fulfillment automation, cost-to-serve) and Management (OP1/OP2, WBRs, input metrics), used to exploit and reduce costs. Horizons 2 and 3 depend on the dynamic capabilities: Development (custom silicon, generative AI, robotics, satellite technology) and Transaction (new commercial models: agent-mediated selling, healthcare subscriptions, connectivity pricing), used to explore and create value. Two capability gaps deserve explicit development: first, a new form of Transaction capability for a world where the buyer may be an AI agent rather than a human on Amazon's page, negotiating terms, data access and fees with external agent platforms; second, regulatory-institutional capability, since the FTC case and the DMA make the design of the marketplace itself an object of innovation. The general rule from class applies: as the value chain position shifts, the whole set of capabilities must be revisited.

2.6 Investment and Expected Return

The investment scale is unprecedented: capital expenditures of roughly US$200 billion planned for 2026, directed mostly to AI and AWS infrastructure, on top of the industry's largest R&D budget (the "technology and infrastructure" line approaching US$100 billion per year). The expected return differs by horizon, which is what makes the plan financeable. Horizon 1 investments return quickly and measurably: robotics reduces cost per unit delivered, advertising converts existing traffic into high-margin revenue, and efficiency gains fund lower prices, spinning the flywheel. Horizon 2 investments return through new profit pools: AWS AI workloads (anchored by Anthropic's multi-gigawatt Trainium commitment), healthcare recurring revenue and agent-originated sales. Horizon 3 investments are valued as real options, Kuiper and autonomous delivery may not pay back this decade, but they protect the ecosystem's future and can each become a new AWS-sized business. The corporate logic remains the one Amazon has followed since 1997: returns are reinvested into prices, delivery and innovation rather than distributed, accepting short-term margin pressure in exchange for long-term free-cash-flow per share, with AWS and advertising financing every other front.

3. Proposed Innovation Management Structure

3.1 Innovation Leadership

The innovation management strategy for Amazon needs to be based on its current approach of small groups with clear ownership. Amazon uses two-pizza teams that focus on specific products or services. Single-threaded leaders help make decisions and overcome barriers (Amazon Web Services, n.d.). We suggest using a designated leader for each major innovation initiative, especially when a project involves more than one business unit. Senior leaders would set portfolio priorities, provide funding and make important decisions that cannot be easily reversed.

Amazon’s recent attempt to decrease the number of organizational layers makes clear ownership more relevant. In September 2024, Andy Jassy requested that each S-team organization increase the ratio of individual contributors to managers by at least 15% by the end of the first quarter of 2025. He also launched a “Bureaucracy Mailbox” to identify unnecessary processes (Amazon, 2024). Reducing layers may help teams make decisions faster, but it also increases the need to coordinate projects involving several teams. Therefore, we suggest using a designated leader for each cross-business project, setting common goals and documenting key decisions.

Leadership also has a responsibility to support both mature and exploratory businesses. While retail and logistics projects may show results relatively quickly, initiatives such as Amazon Leo require substantial investment before their commercial potential can be fully assessed. Senior leaders should evaluate each type of project using criteria appropriate to its stage of development.

3.2 Teams and Responsibilities

The four capabilities outlined in class help identify what each project requires. Development capability produces new technologies, such as AI systems, custom chips and robotics. Operational capability turns them into reliable services at scale. Managerial capability sets priorities and coordinates resources. Finally, transaction capability helps Amazon create value through its relationships with customers, sellers, developers and partners. These are capabilities to develop across the company, rather than four new reporting divisions.

Small teams should bring together the capabilities needed to address a specific customer problem. An AI shopping initiative, for example, would require input from retail, AI infrastructure, Marketplace and Advertising. The team would use Amazon’s Working Backwards process to define the desired customer experience. Its leader would coordinate the participating units and resolve decisions affecting more than one of them.

Amazon will have to evaluate how tight the control has to be for each activity depending on the strategic significance and asset specificity of each activity. Some activities might be so specific that they need a tight internal coordination, whereas some other activities can be carried out using the competencies of external parties. Amazon Leo is one example of such an arrangement.

3.3 Pace of Innovation

Amazon ought to maintain its innovation proactiveness strategy, but give room for the different paces of development. This should vary depending on technological uncertainties, investment and reversibility of decision making, but not just the time horizon of the project.

The digital innovations can be continuously tested and fine-tuned. Alexa for Shopping (earlier known as Rufus) has become more powerful with successive updates. This can be measured with the use of the customers and their shopping experience. Innovation projects related to chips, robotics technology, and satellites have to follow gradual investment and evaluation process. At first, it can be done in relation to feasibility and performance; later, adoption and profitability.

At all these stages, the leader responsible should decide whether to move on, change course or discontinue the project. The decision to move forward, change course or discontinue the project should be taken in the light of Amazon's classification of decisions that can be reversed versus those that cannot be reversed.

3.4 Simultaneity and Project Coordination

Amazon needs to improve its current operations while developing new sources of growth. This strategy is consistent with the ambidextrous organization discussed by O’Reilly and Tushman (2016): exploratory projects have dedicated leadership and room to experiment, but can also use assets from established businesses. An AI shopping project could benefit from Amazon’s retail experience and AWS infrastructure. Amazon Leo could connect its service to AWS.

We suggest reviewing projects across all three horizons as a portfolio. This review should consider each project’s contribution to Amazon’s strategy, evidence of progress, required capabilities and demand for shared resources such as computing capacity, engineering talent and funding. Established businesses would be evaluated mainly through customer and operational outcomes. Exploratory initiatives would also be evaluated through technical and market-learning milestones.

The review should identify duplication and dependencies among autonomous teams. Amazon itself recognizes that small, independent teams can become isolated or repeat one another’s work (Amazon Web Services, n.d.). Cross-business leaders could address these problems while leaving day-to-day product decisions with the teams closest to customers. Finally, projects affecting Marketplace and Advertising should include an early review of regulatory implications, given the scrutiny these services face in the United States and Europe.
